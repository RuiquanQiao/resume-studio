# Planning with a student

This is the method. It is not a template: the plan's shape still comes from what the user tells you (see
the Plan notes in Resume Studio's `SKILL.md`). What this file adds is what a good adviser knows and an
anxious student often doesn't: **which dates decide the plan**, what the tests actually test, and how to
turn "I'm behind" into one thing to do today.

## 1. Before anything else: today's date against the calendar

The worst plan is one aimed at a window that has already closed. Every time you write or rework a plan:

1. Take today's date from the environment. Never assume the month.
2. Work out where the user stands in each market they could apply in (section 2): which windows are **open
   now**, which **have closed** this cycle, and which **open next**, with the month.
3. Put the nearest live deadline in the plan, with what must be ready by then. If a window the user is
   counting on has closed or doesn't fit them, say so in the chat, plainly, once, with the
   alternative. This is the single most useful thing you can tell them.
4. The months below are the usual pattern, not this year's dates. Before you put a date in the plan, find
   the employer's own page for this cycle (WebSearch, then WebFetch). Write the date with its source, or
   write "usually around <month>, check". Never invent a date, a quota or a pass rate.

## 2. The calendars

### China (校招)

- **届 (graduating class)** is decided by the **date on the degree certificate**. Coursework ending, the
  thesis defence and the ceremony don't count. Each company sets its own window, and for overseas degrees
  it is often wider. Examples for 2027届, read 2026-10:
  - Baidu: degrees from 2026-09 to 2027-08.
  - Huawei: overseas degrees from 2026-01 to 2027-12.
  - OPPO: overseas degrees from 2026-09 to 2027-12.
  - The 留学服务中心 doesn't define 应届 for returnees; each employer does. Check each target company's
    rules, and whether it needs the 留服认证 (degree verification) at onboarding.
- **One 届 has one main cycle, and it starts a year before graduation:**
  - **暑期实习** (summer internship): applications around **March–May**, the internship around June–August,
    usually with a return offer (转正). It is for the class graduating the **following** year, so a 2027届
    student's 暑期实习 was spring 2026. It is often the easiest way into a big company.
  - **秋招提前批** (early round): big internet companies start around **June–August**. It is fast, and
    there are fewer applicants.
  - **正式秋招** (main round): most employers open around **September–October**. It is the largest round.
  - **补录** (late hiring): around **November–December**, for places still unfilled.
  - **春招** (spring round): around **February–April** of the graduation year. It is smaller and mostly
    fills gaps, though some employers (state-owned firms in particular) recruit in earnest.
  - **日常实习 / 寒假实习** (rolling or winter internships): year-round, applied for directly or through a
    referral. These are the route for someone who missed 暑期实习. Ask whether the user could intern
    remotely or go back to China during a break.
- **What is tested** (for technical roles):
  - an online written test (笔试): ACM-style problems on 牛客 or 赛码, where you read input and print output
    yourself; often there are also multiple-choice questions on CS basics;
  - live coding in interviews (手撕), usually LeetCode easy–medium;
  - 八股 (standard knowledge questions): data structures, OS, networks, databases, and the language in depth;
  - **项目深挖** (a deep dive into the projects): the interviewer asks why each design choice was made and
    what failed. A project the user can't explain hurts more than having no project;
  - for algorithm or ML roles, add: deriving the core models, ML fundamentals, and the papers the user cites.

### Australia

- **Summer internships / vacationer programs** run over the summer break, about **November to February**,
  and are mostly for **penultimate-year** students. Applications for one summer open and **close several
  months before it**.
  - Example for 2026/27: Macquarie took applications from 5 May 2026 until 21 July (Melbourne and Perth) or
    4 August 2026 (Sydney and Brisbane), for a program starting November 2026.
  - So by September most big programs for the coming summer have closed. What is still open later: smaller
    companies, startups, off-cycle internships, research assistant work at the user's own university
    (ask a lecturer), and roles posted one at a time.
- **Graduate programs** recruit about **a year before the start date**.
  - Examples: Canva's February 2027 intake took applications from 4 May to 6 June 2026. DFAT's 2027
    program closed on 12 April 2026.
  - Penultimate-year interns are often offered a graduate place, so the summer internship is the route into
    the graduate program.
- **Work rights decide eligibility. Check them first:**
  - Many government and defence roles, and some banks, require citizenship or PR.
  - Many tech companies take international students with full work rights after graduation but **don't
    sponsor visas**. Canva's early-talent roles, for example, sponsor none.
  - On a student visa: at most **48 hours per fortnight** while a course is in session, and unlimited
    hours in scheduled course breaks.
  - After graduating: the **Temporary Graduate visa (485)** has a post-higher-education stream. For a
    coursework master's it lasts 2 years, and the applicant must be under 35. Check the Home Affairs
    page for the current rules before you plan on it.
- **What is tested:**
  - an online assessment (HackerRank or Codility style);
  - a recorded video interview;
  - behavioural questions, answered with STAR (situation, task, action, result);
  - a technical interview;
  - for large programs, an assessment centre.

### The university year

- Exams and assignment weeks take time away from the plan. Put them in the plan as weeks with less
  in them, not as weeks of failure.
- The calendar can conflict with the hiring calendar. For example, China's 正式秋招 falls in Australia's
  semester 2, which ends with exams in November. Ask for the user's real dates.
- Australian universities mostly run semester 1 about February–June and semester 2 about July–November,
  so the summer break is about December–February. Check the user's own university.

### Another market (US, UK, Hong Kong, Singapore, Japan…)

Work out its calendar the same way before you plan for it: find who is eligible, when applications open,
and when it is too late. Use the employers' own pages and cite them.

## 3. Where they stand: measure it, don't take it on faith

Anxious students tend to rate themselves lower than they are, or higher. Neither is a plan.

- Ask only what the plan depends on:
  - the target roles and markets;
  - their 届 or visa position;
  - how many hours a week they can really give, and what already fills their week;
  - their deadlines.
  Ask in small numbers; don't send a questionnaire.
- **Find their level by doing something small together**, not with a rating scale:
  - one easy problem in their main language, solved without looking anything up;
  - explaining one of their own projects: what each part does and why;
  - one piece of maths the target role really uses.
  Write down what happened ("two-pointer easy: solved, 25 min, needed help with input parsing"). Don't
  write a score.
- Name the real constraint. Is it time, skill, a story they can tell about their projects, or
  eligibility? Usually one of them dominates.

## 4. The easiest way forward

Work back from the nearest live deadline in section 1, and from what that test asks for.

- **Choose one language** for problems and interviews, and stay with it.
- **Learn only what the role tests, in the order it is tested.** First the written test and live coding
  (problem patterns, fluency with input and output), then the role's standard questions, then the
  project deep dive. Maths only as far as the role's interview needs it. Relearning a whole subject is a
  plan for later, if they still want it.
- **Make the projects they have their own** before building new ones. A vibe-coded project becomes an
  asset once they can explain it, rebuild its core alone, and say what they would change. Interviewers
  dig into projects, so this is the fastest gain for the story the user tells about themselves.
- **Write down what is left out for now, and why.** A short "not now" list lowers anxiety more than a
  long to-do list.
- Give the reason for each choice in the plan, so the next version can tell what changed.

## 5. Commitments they can keep

- Use few of them, and keep them small and checkable: "2 problems a day from the two-pointer list, without
  looking up syntax" can be checked; "get better at algorithms" can't. Make each one a `- [ ]` item or a
  dated line.
- Size them for a normal week, not their best week. A plan that survives a bad week is the one that gets
  followed.
- Tie them to the calendar: each phase should end before the date that needs it.

## 6. Tracking, and changing the plan

- When the user says what they did, log it with `note`. Tick an item only when they say it is done.
- When a chat about the plan starts, compare what they committed to with what is logged.
  - If they are behind, say so once, plainly and kindly, with the gap in facts ("this week: 3 of 10
    problems").
  - Then ask whether to shrink the commitment or move it. Don't lecture, and don't repeat it.
- **Rework the plan** when a deadline passes, when the pace has been off for about two weeks, when the
  target changes, or when the user asks. Rewrite it with `write --why "<what changed and why>"`, so the
  old version stays.

## 7. Tone

- Anxiety eases with clarity about where they stand and what to do next, not with reassurance. End each
  plan conversation with **the next concrete thing to do today or tomorrow**.
- Be honest about closed windows and real gaps, and give the route that is still open in the same breath.
- Don't promise outcomes, don't invent figures, and don't compare them with other students.
- Use the user's own language and keep their words.
